Research

Which road segments will become impassable, when, and who loses access to emergency services?

Before a flash flood, a district engineer has no tool that answers this question road by road. This research builds one for central Massachusetts and tests it on real storms.

The problem

Warnings describe areas, not roads

Flash floods close roads quickly and with little warning, and storms of this kind are becoming more frequent in the Northeast. National Weather Service alerts cover whole areas, not street segments, and global flood forecasts work on cells about 20 km wide. Existing road flood-risk methods rate roads in advance from fixed conditions; they cannot say which segments will go under in a coming storm. Newer network studies either need the flood to be underway already or have not been tested on storms they were not built on.

Closures also spread beyond the flooded road: detours fail, and some closures come from cascading failures such as embankment washouts rather than standing water.

Research question 1

From forecast or radar rainfall, can a physics-based model identify which road segments become impassable (about 30 cm of water) in the next 0–24 hours, and how well does it do on storms it was not tuned on?

Research question 2

How do individual closures combine across the road network to cut residents off from hospitals, fire and police, and how large is that impact compared with the flooded roads themselves?

Research question 3

What limits prediction skill, and what is gained by adding better labels, learned models and cascading-failure mechanisms?

Objectives

Research objectives

1

Road-level hazard

Compute water depth on every road segment of MassDOT District 3 on a 20 m grid from NOAA radar rainfall and HRRR forecasts, and convert depth to a closure likelihood using a 30 cm passability threshold and culvert capacity checks.

2

Independent validation

Evaluate the model with settings fixed and rules set in advance: against river gauges, NWS and NOAA storm reports, city-confirmed closures, storms withheld from calibration and storms in other districts, followed by a one-year prospective test on real storms.

3

Network consequence and resilience

Quantify the loss of emergency access (hospital within 30 min, fire within 8 min, police within 10 min) and added detour delay, using the change in delay as the network resilience measure (Ganin et al., 2017) alongside section-level pavement resilience (Nivedya et al., 2018).

4

Cascading mechanisms and parametric study

Once the depth model is validated, add the failure mechanisms that water depth alone misses (culvert overtopping and blockage, embankment washout, debris), and test how much each condition changes predicted closures, such as clogged versus clear culverts and dry versus saturated or frozen soil.

5

Decision tool

Deliver a per-segment closure forecast and vulnerability rating that a district can use before a storm, in the ArcGIS environment MassDOT uses.

Practical application

Decisions the forecast supports

MassDOT District 3 is where the work is developed and tested. Today the district prepares with area forecasts and its staff's knowledge of known trouble spots. District 3 staff named only three places where closures are common, and said it would be valuable to learn which places may be affected in the future that staff have not flagged.

Preparedness

Before a storm: where to stage crews, barricades and signs, and which detours to plan, including detours that may flood themselves.

Operations and maintenance

Which culverts and drainage inlets to inspect and clear first. Comparing clogged and clear culverts in the model shows where cleaning changes the outcome most.

Rehabilitation

Which segments flood again and again across storms, and are candidates for better drainage or a raised roadway.

Budget

What each closure costs in lost travel time and emergency access, so maintenance and capital spending can be ranked by the harm they avoid.

Model status

A segment-level screen, tested on independent storms

Relative to current practice, area-based warnings and staff knowledge of recurring problem sites, the model provides storm-specific, segment-level guidance together with its consequences for emergency access.

Scope

Observed and forecast rainfall is translated into two-dimensional flood depths at 20 m resolution. Each road segment is evaluated against a passability threshold, and closures are propagated through the road network to estimate the loss of access to hospitals, fire stations and police. The system runs automatically ahead of and during storms.

Evidence

On storms withheld from calibration, segments with reported flooding are concentrated among the model's highest-ranked roads, well above a random baseline. The model is most useful as a street-scale prioritisation tool, including at sites not previously identified by district staff.

Limitations

Outputs are a risk ranking rather than a calibrated warning for an individual segment. Storm drainage, culvert blockage during a storm, embankment failure and antecedent ground conditions (saturated or frozen soil) are not yet represented, and validation is constrained by incomplete records of road closures.

Development

Incorporation of culvert condition and antecedent soil moisture, explicit cascading-failure mechanisms, a twelve-month prospective forecast evaluation, and delivery of segment forecasts within MassDOT's GIS environment.

Contributions

What this work adds

Segment-level forecasting

A forecast-driven, physics-based screen that names the road segments likely to close in the next 0–24 hours, not just the area at risk.

Independent evaluation

Settings fixed on one storm, rules written down before testing, and every result judged on storms the model never saw, next to what chance alone would give.

Network consequences

Closures carried through to lost hospital, fire and police access, with the change in travel delay as a measure of network resilience.

Cascading failure

Shows where water depth alone misses closures: washouts, debris and clogged culverts.

Agency application

Developed for MassDOT District 3, to be delivered in its mapping tools and tested over a year of real storms.

Relation to existing work

  • Road flood-risk ratings such as the hazard–vulnerability–consequence risk factor (Tao and Mallick, 2020; Chen et al., 2024) rate roads in advance from fixed conditions. This work says which segments go under in a particular coming storm.
  • Flash-flood forecasts from national and global systems work on areas or cells about 20 km wide (Zlydenko et al., 2026). This work works at the scale of a street.
  • Network and learned-model studies of flooded roads (Yuan et al., 2021; Dong et al., 2026; Shariatfar et al., 2026) need the flood to be underway, or are checked on one storm, often outside the Northeast. This work starts from the rain and is tested on many storms it was not built on, in inland New England.
  • Records of flooded roads are incomplete: a road with no report was not necessarily dry, and reports come more often from busy places (Safaei-Moghadam et al., 2024; Praharaj et al., 2021). This work treats them as a limit to measure, not as complete truth.

How we check it

Tested on storms it has never seen

Rules written first

Every test is written down and dated before the model is run, so results cannot be tuned after the fact.

Unseen storms

The model is set up on one storm and then judged on past storms it was never adjusted to, in central Massachusetts and beyond.

Against what agencies use today

Its road list is compared with flood warnings, flood-zone maps and simpler rules, using real reports of flooded roads.

Against river gauges

Modelled water levels are compared with U.S. Geological Survey stream gauges for every storm.
