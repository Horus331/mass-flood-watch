Which roads a flash flood will close, when, and why

Mass Flood Watch turns radar rainfall into a road-by-road picture of flash-flood closures for MassDOT Highway District 3 in central Massachusetts: where water reaches the road, when each segment closes and reopens, and who loses access to a hospital, fire station or police station while it is shut.

What it does

For a storm that happened (a replay), a storm that is forecast (the live forecast, rerun every six hours from NOAA HRRR) or a storm with changed inputs (the what-if explorer), the app shows the water as the flood model moves it across the terrain, every road segment as it gets wet, risky and closed, the culverts it overtops, and the people cut off from emergency services. Every map is one physics run; nothing on it is drawn by hand.

How it works

  1. Rain

    NOAA MRMS radar-and-gauge rainfall, 1 km, every hour of the storm.

  2. Flood model

    LISFLOOD-FP 8.2 spreads the rain over USGS 3DEP lidar terrain at 20 m. Soils and land cover set how much soaks in. Validated runs use one converged solver setting for every storm.

  3. Water on every road

    Depth on each road segment every 5 to 30 minutes: when water arrives, how deep, and for how long.

  4. Closure rules

    30 cm of water stops an ordinary car, so the chance of closing rises steeply past 30 cm. A stream reaching road level marks a road that may close. Culverts are checked against FHWA HDS-5.

  5. Network impact

    Residents from the 2020 Census who lose a hospital within 30 min, a fire station within 8 min or police within 10 min; detour delay; risk class and repair cost.

Every run is scored against what happened: USGS river gauges, NWS storm reports, NOAA Storm Events, and documented city and MassDOT closure and damage sites, each compared with random road points.

    On 81 storms in MassDOT Districts 4, 5 and 6 (498 documented sites), run once with the frozen District 3 settings, the top 3% of road segments catch 21% of the sites (104 of 498) against 8.6% by chance: a lift of 2.5. The 30 cm alert rule rarely fires there (18 of 498).

  • Against FEMA flood maps

    On the 23 held-out storms, FEMA's 100-year flood zones flag 7.0% of roads and catch 32 of 98 sites; the model's riskiest roads at the same share catch 36 of 98. The difference (−0.04, 90% CI −0.18 to +0.11) is not detectable, so a static map is a fair benchmark for where; the model adds when each road closes and a sharper alert rule (lift 6.4 against 2.2).

The research Draft: Mo to check

The dissertation builds a practical framework for cascading water hazards on infrastructure: heavy rain becomes flooding, flooding closes and damages roads and culverts, and those failures cut people off from help. The questions it asks:

  1. Can a physics flood model driven by radar rain say which road segments close, and when, well enough to beat chance on storms it was never tuned on?
  2. How do the consequences cascade through the network: culverts overtopped, detours, and residents cut off from hospitals, fire and police?
  3. Can forecast rain give useful hours of warning, and can machine learning (a learned closure model, a fast surrogate of the flood model) add skill or speed without hiding what the physics says?
  4. Which inputs matter most: rain, how wet the ground was, and whether culverts pass water (the what-if explorer)?

Limitations

  • Light storms are under-predicted. The runoff setting tuned on Leominster leaves summer thunderstorms too dry: the alert rule catches 1 of 58 sites on the 16 newer storms. Drains, 5-minute rain and connected-pavement runoff were each tested and none fixed it.
  • Spatial skill outside the development storms is at chance. On held-out storms the model ranks roads better than chance but does not place the flooded area (FSS at 300 m: 0.019 against 0.234 on the development storms).
  • Labels follow who reported. Outside Leominster, closure sites come from news, NWS and agency reports, which favour named main roads and towns that report. An unreported road is not proof it stayed open, so a flagged road with no report is not necessarily a false alarm, and storms with 2 to 7 sites carry wide uncertainty.
  • 20 m grid. Curbs, gutters and narrow ditches are smaller than one cell, so water on city streets is smoothed out.
  • River floods need basin-scale runs. Large rivers drain land outside the model and peak after the 48-hour window (see the gauge table).
  • Forecast tested on few sites. The ensemble’s out-of-sample test rests on 29 sites across 7 storms, so one site moves a storm’s recall by 0.2 to 0.5.
