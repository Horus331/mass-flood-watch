Which roads a flash flood will close, when, and why

Mass Flood Watch turns radar rainfall into a road-by-road picture of flash-flood closures for MassDOT Highway District 3 in central Massachusetts: where water reaches the road, when each segment closes and reopens, and who loses access to a hospital, fire station or police station while it is shut.

What it does

For a storm that happened (a replay), a storm that is forecast (the live forecast, rerun every six hours from NOAA HRRR) or a storm with changed inputs (the what-if explorer), the app shows the water as the flood model moves it across the terrain, every road segment as it gets wet, risky and closed, the culverts it overtops, and the people cut off from emergency services. Every map is one physics run; nothing on it is drawn by hand.

Reading the map

Everything on the map is model output or a measurement; nothing is drawn by hand. The timeline under the map plays the storm: drag anywhere on it, or use the arrow keys (Shift and an arrow moves an hour). Slow, Normal and Fast play 5, 15 and 45 minutes of storm per second.

Water depth
Modelled water on the ground, from 5 cm (pale) to 1 m and more (dark). 15 cm reaches a car floor; 30 cm can float a small car.
Draining
Slate-grey water is going down: draining downstream or soaking in.
Rain
NOAA MRMS radar rain in mm per hour: light (0.5), moderate (2.5), heavy (10), very heavy (25), violent (50).
Water on road
5 to 15 cm of modelled water on the road surface.
At risk
15 to 30 cm.
Closed
30 cm or more on the segment: the closure rule. A closed road reopens when the water drops below 26 cm, so a depth hovering at the threshold does not flicker.
Impassable stretch
Red marks only the 40 m pieces where 30 cm or more covers the road itself, so one deep spot does not paint a whole kilometre red.
Water at the edge
Water beside the road while the road surface (an embankment or a crowned road) stays above it.
May close
A stream beside the road reaches road level; not counted in the chance of closing.
Coverage
The outline is MassDOT District 3, where the live forecast runs. Dashed boxes elsewhere were tested on past storms only; the shaded area outside is not modelled. On a replay, the dashed square is that run's own 20 × 20 km box.

The three numbers beside the map are the road segments closed and at risk at the moment shown on the timeline, and the time when the most roads are closed at once. Rain now and culverts overtopped are area values at the same moment. Click a road, on the map or in the list, for its depth through the storm, when it closes and reopens, its traffic (AADT) and the residents who lose access to a hospital, fire or police station if it closes. The guided tour (Tour) walks through the storm in eight scenes with the same numbers.

The default basemap is the MassGIS 2023 statewide aerial imagery (15 cm, leaf-off, so roads under tree canopy are visible), with Esri World Imagery outside Massachusetts. Light, Terrain and Dark backgrounds are under Layers. Moving water (Layers, at street zoom) shows the direction and relative speed of the modelled current, not to time scale.

The storms

  • Leominster, Sept 11, 2023: the tuned case. A 20 × 20 km box at 20 m; the storm the model settings were checked against.
  • Worcester, Aug 19, 2021: a blind test. Same model settings, on a storm it was not tuned on.
  • Eight more storms (2018 to 2026, under More storms): held-out replays. Same settings, none chosen on them. Each entry gives the most rain in the box and the most in one hour.
  • District 3, Sept 11, 2023: one run over the whole district on WPI’s Turing GPU cluster, 17.6 million cells at 20 m (shown at 80 m).

Scenarios

The Scenarios tab reruns the real Leominster 2023 storm with one input changed at a time: rain scaled to ½, ¾ or 1¼ of the radar storm; the ground before the storm dry (the driest soil class), normal (the calibrated setting), soaked (the wettest soil class) or frozen (nothing soaks in, an upper bound); and culverts clear or blocked (road embankments then hold water back like dams; bridges stay open). Each combination is a full LISFLOOD-FP run on WPI’s Turing GPU cluster, not an extrapolation. A combination marked with a dot is still queued, and the map keeps the last run that has landed. People cut off counts residents with no route to a hospital, fire or police station at the worst moment.

How it works

  1. Rain

    NOAA MRMS radar-and-gauge rainfall, 1 km, every hour of the storm.

  2. Flood model

    LISFLOOD-FP 8.2 spreads the rain over USGS 3DEP lidar terrain at 20 m. Soils and land cover set how much soaks in. Validated runs use one converged solver setting for every storm.

  3. Water on every road

    Depth on each road segment every 5 to 30 minutes: when water arrives, how deep, and for how long.

  4. Closure rules

    30 cm of water stops an ordinary car, so the chance of closing rises steeply past 30 cm. A stream reaching road level marks a road that may close. Culverts are checked against FHWA HDS-5.

  5. Network impact

    Residents from the 2020 Census who lose a hospital within 30 min, a fire station within 8 min or police within 10 min; detour delay; risk class and repair cost.

Every run is scored against what happened: USGS river gauges, NWS storm reports, NOAA Storm Events, and documented city and MassDOT closure and damage sites, each compared with random road points.

  • New districts, no retuning

    On 81 storms in MassDOT Districts 4, 5 and 6 (498 documented sites), run once with the frozen District 3 settings, the top 3% of road segments catch 21% of the sites (104 of 498) against 8.6% by chance: a lift of 2.5. The 30 cm alert rule rarely fires there (18 of 498).

    Transfer test

  • Against FEMA flood maps

    On the 23 held-out storms, FEMA's 100-year flood zones flag 7.0% of roads and catch 32 of 98 sites; the model's riskiest roads at the same share catch 36 of 98. The difference (−0.04, 90% CI −0.18 to +0.11) is not detectable, so a static map is a fair benchmark for where; the model adds when each road closes and a sharper alert rule (lift 6.4 against 2.2).

    Static flood maps vs the event model

  • Model plus FEMA map, tested and rejected

    Adding FEMA's flood zones to the alert rule (pre-registered, Districts 4–6, 81 storms) caught more sites than the rule alone but fewer than the model's own riskiest roads at the same share, so no hybrid layer is used.

    Hybrid test

  • Fast learned stand-in for the flood model

    A neural network trained on 192 flood-model runs on the WPI Turing cluster, meant to answer what-if questions in seconds. Its first validation is weak (road-level agreement 0.34 at 30 cm, against a 0.70 bar set in advance), so it is not used anywhere on this site. In development

    Surrogate plan

  • Street inlets, tested and rejected

    Catch basins taking water off the street, capped by grate capacity with leaf and debris clogging (FHWA HEC-22), pre-registered and run on the 23 held-out storms: the share of documented sites caught in the riskiest 3% of roads stayed at 0.194 with and without inlets, so they stay off. Drained water is not yet returned at the outfalls, which made the Leominster river rise worse.

    Inlet test

  • Residents' flood reports, Leominster

    The MRPC / BSC Group crowdsource map has 70 road-related pins from residents. 27 of them are new sites, more than 150 m from any other report, and were never used to build the model: it catches 6 of them (22%) while flagging 2.2% of roads, about 5 times chance.

The research

The dissertation builds a practical framework for cascading water hazards on infrastructure: heavy rain becomes flooding, flooding closes and damages roads and culverts, and those failures cut people off from help. The questions it asks:

  1. Can a physics flood model driven by radar rain say which road segments close, and when, well enough to beat chance on storms it was never tuned on?
  2. How do the consequences cascade through the network: culverts overtopped, detours, and residents cut off from hospitals, fire and police?
  3. Can forecast rain give useful hours of warning, and can machine learning (a learned closure model, a fast surrogate of the flood model) add skill or speed without hiding what the physics says?
  4. Which inputs matter most: rain, how wet the ground was, and whether culverts pass water (the what-if explorer)?

The week-ahead rain table

The Forecast tab shows the next 7 days the way a phone weather app does: one row per day, 12 columns of 2 hours each, the day's total and the chance of rain. The rain comes from NOAA's National Blend of Models, hourly at the 73 District 3 towns through Open-Meteo (open-meteo.com, CC BY 4.0). NOAA forecasts hour by hour for about the next 36 hours; after that its 3- and 6-hour totals are spread over the hours, so later days look smoother until they come closer.

Each column is the rain in the wettest town in those 2 hours, so a local downpour is not averaged away. The dashed line on every column is the flood model's trigger: 25 mm in 6 hours in any town (8.3 mm in 2 hours). Columns below the line use a square-root scale so light rain stays visible; a column that reaches the line turns orange, and once that rain is within 48 hours the flood model runs on the road network. The total is the District 3 average; the chance of rain is NOAA's 12-hour probability of measurable rain (0.25 mm or more) for the day, at the most likely spot in District 3. This table is for orientation; road closures come only from the flood model.

Week-ahead rain is uncertain, and road closures are modelled only within 48 hours of a storm (NOAA HRRR). “Ground before the next storm” is context: the forecast runs at normal (calibrated) ground, so wet or frozen ground means closures are likely under-forecast. “Right now” lists observations and short-range products, checked every hour: radar rain, a 2-hour radar extrapolation (experimental, not yet verified), flash-flood guidance, river gauges against flood stage, National Water Model flow at culverts, snow, and recent flooding reports.

Limitations

  • Light storms are under-predicted. The runoff setting tuned on Leominster leaves summer thunderstorms too dry: the alert rule catches 1 of 58 sites on the 16 newer storms. Drains, 5-minute rain, connected-pavement runoff and (1 Nov) dropping the extra 2 mm/h soil loss on top of the curve number were each tested and none fixed it (the last: 2 of 58, not significant; test).
  • Spatial skill outside the development storms is at chance. On held-out storms the model ranks roads better than chance but does not place the flooded area (FSS at 300 m: 0.019 against 0.234 on the development storms).
  • Labels follow who reported. Outside Leominster, closure sites come from news, NWS and agency reports, which favour named main roads and towns that report. An unreported road is not proof it stayed open, so a flagged road with no report is not necessarily a false alarm, and storms with 2 to 7 sites carry wide uncertainty.
  • Model start-up. Each run starts with streams partly full; in the first half hour that water drains through some crossings onto roads before any real rain falls. The map ignores road water until 5 mm of rain has fallen on the area, and then counts a road as closed only if its water is 30 cm or more and at least 15 cm above that start-up level.
  • Known artifacts. In downtown Worcester (MLK Jr Blvd, Foster St) the model holds 1 to 4 m of water in low spots with no outlet: Mill Brook runs in a conduit under the city and the terrain shows the streets and buildings over it, so those roads rank too high. A few culvert crossings are cut far below the road where several crossings sit close together (Route 2 at I-190 in Leominster shows 8.5 m; it is not a bridge). A fix for both was built and tested on all 25 storms (1 Nov): it removes these depths (no road deeper than 2 m except one) without changing the skill scores, and was adopted with the advisors' approval on 5 Nov 2026: the live forecast uses it from that day, and since 7 Nov 2026 the storm replays on this site are rebuilt with it too (test). Rivers and ponds are drawn on the map only where they rise at least 20 cm above their normal level during the storm, so a river out of its banks shows and a full lake does not. The live forecast also measures water on a road from the moment the rain begins, so low spots that are already full before a storm are not counted as closures. Before October 2020 the radar layer on the map shows radar-only rain, which reads 30 to 40% lower than the gauge-corrected rain the model was run with.
  • 20 m grid. Curbs, gutters and narrow ditches are smaller than one cell, so water on city streets is smoothed out.
  • River floods need basin-scale runs. Large rivers drain land outside the model and peak after the 48-hour window (see the gauge table).
  • Forecast tested on few sites. The ensemble’s out-of-sample test rests on 29 sites across 7 storms, so one site moves a storm’s recall by 0.2 to 0.5.
